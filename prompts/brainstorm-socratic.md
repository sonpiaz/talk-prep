# Socratic brainstorm prompt

Use this prompting pattern when guiding the speaker through **Phase 2 (IDEAS)** of the workflow. The goal is to surface 20–30 rough ideas from the speaker's own life, not to invent generic talk content.

## Frame this session out loud

> "I am not writing your talk. I am going to ask you questions and write down what falls out. Answer in one or two sentences. If you do not know, skip. We want 20–30 raw ideas in 15 minutes, not 3 polished ones."

Then work through the six probe categories below. Ask one at a time. When the speaker gives an answer, dump it straight into `01-ideas.md` under the right section — do not paraphrase.

---

## Probe 1 — Failures with numbers

Specific failures are more memorable than generic lessons. Numbers make them feel real.

- "What was the biggest project you shipped that failed? How much money and how many months did it cost?"
- "What is a project you are still paying for emotionally? What did it teach you?"
- "What did you spend a year on that you would not spend a year on again?"
- "Who told you it would not work, and you went ahead anyway?"

## Probe 2 — Hidden stats

Numbers the audience could never guess about the speaker or the field.

- "In the last 30 days, how many of X have you done? (commits, calls, posts, lines of code, sales)"
- "What is the ratio that surprises you about your own work? (e.g., 1 hit out of 89 tries)"
- "Is there a number that feels embarrassingly high or low to say out loud? Say it."

## Probe 3 — Beliefs the speaker has abandoned

Paradigm shifts are the spine of good founder talks. "I used to believe X. I no longer believe X."

- "What is something you believed strongly 2 years ago that you no longer believe?"
- "What is a rule everyone in your field still follows that you broke, and nothing bad happened?"
- "What advice do you now give that you would have called naive three years ago?"

## Probe 4 — Quotes that hit you

Not LinkedIn-quote-of-the-day. Quotes the speaker actually thinks about.

- "Whose sentence lives rent-free in your head this year?"
- "Quote a specific line from a specific person (with year + context if possible)."
- "Is there a quote you disagree with now that you agreed with before?"

## Probe 5 — Vivid scenes / objects

Image-first ideas that can carry a slide visually.

- "If this talk were a film, what is the opening shot?"
- "What single object represents the old way, and what object represents the new way?"
- "What did your desk look like the day everything changed?"

## Probe 6 — Boring-but-true

What everyone in the room feels but nobody says.

- "What do you all complain about in private but pretend is fine in public?"
- "What is the 'bad take' that happens to be true?"
- "What does everybody in this field do, that they would quietly admit does not actually work?"

---

## After the probes

Count the raw ideas. If you have fewer than 20, pick the two probe categories with the fewest entries and ask 2–3 more follow-ups in each.

Do not edit or rank yet. Leave raw. The next phase (outline) is where you rank.

## Anti-patterns to avoid

- Do not ask "what is your big idea?" — founders stall on this question. Use concrete probes.
- Do not paraphrase the speaker's words into "cleaner" versions. The raw phrasing often contains the line that will land on stage.
- Do not steer toward a conclusion you already have in mind. The shape of the talk comes later.
- Do not use ideas from other talks you have seen. The audience can tell.
